What the data shows

Five things the AwareMind data shows

The analysis uses all 5,772 in-session check-ins from 37 US adults (Zheng & Choe, 2026a). A high-gap moment means leaning toward “stop” (an answer below 5.5) and then using the phone for more than 5 more minutes. Figures are numbered as in the report.

14.9%
of check-ins were high-gap moments (859 of 5,772), matching the paper’s published figure exactly
Report: Insight 1 · Figure 1

Most check-ins lean toward continuing, but the gap is a substantial minority. 72% of answers leaned toward continuing, and 29.4% were a firm 10. Further use was heavily skewed: a median of 5.1 minutes, while 5.2% of check-ins ran past an hour.

Supplementary analysis
Leaning toward “stop” barely changed what happened next: 52.6% of those check-ins were followed by more than 5 more minutes of use, against 50.9% when leaning toward “continue”. Only a firm 1 usually ended the session (26.7%). After an answer of 3, the median further use was 12 minutes.

33.7%
high-gap share between midnight and 6 am (n = 395), against 12.6% from 9 am to 6 pm
Report: Insight 2 · Figure 2

The gap peaks at night, reaching 45% at 3 am (n = 49, with a wide confidence interval). Weekends (15.4%) barely differ from weekdays (14.7%). This matches the finding that usage profiles with lower well-being are marked by night-time sessions (Katevas et al., 2018). Time of day is known before the outcome, so a real-time model can use it.

23.7%
high-gap share in social media apps (n = 908), against 10.6% in messaging
Report: Insight 3 · Figure 3

Social media carries the largest gap, followed by media and video (18.4%) and browsers (16.2%). System tools are lowest (4.4%). This agrees with one sec users choosing to restrict social media most often (Haliburton et al., 2024). The app category is missing for 12.4% of check-ins.

13% → 22%
high-gap share from the first 30 seconds of a session to more than 10 minutes in
Report: Insight 4 · Figure 4

The gap grows as a session goes on (13.4% → 21.6%), so elapsed time is a useful real-time signal. The participants are heavy users: at least 3.7 hours of screen time on the median participant-day, rising with age (3.0, 3.6 and 4.6 hours).

0.44
intraclass correlation: about 44% of the gap’s variation lies between people
Report: Insight 5 · Figure 5

The gap is strongly person-specific. Individual high-gap shares range from 0% to 97.2% (median 7.0%). This explains why one shared model barely works for new users (R² = .035; Zheng & Choe, 2026b), and it makes personalization the core of NudgeWell.

What the literature says

14 studies, four themes, four gaps

Part 1 of the report reviews 14 studies in four themes. Each card gives a theme’s main takeaway; the coral cards are the gaps NudgeWell addresses.

Measurement
Scales measure people, not moments

Self-reported use only moderately matches logged use (Parry et al., 2021). The SAS-SV scale has a validated Turkish version (Noyan et al., 2015).

Consequences
Patterns matter more than minutes

In 10,099 US adults, objectively measured use showed weak or null links with mood (Winbush et al., 2025). Lower-well-being profiles were marked by night-time use (Katevas et al., 2018).

Interventions
Brief nudges work, then fade

Short frictions made app openings more intentional for 1,039 one sec users, who then took breaks and rebounded (Haliburton et al., 2024). TypeOut cut app usage by more than half (Xu et al., 2022).

AI
Models find problem users, not moments

Time2Stop learns when to intervene (Orzikulova et al., 2024). Only AwareMind predicts the gap itself, and its model barely works for new users (Zheng & Choe, 2026b).

  • MeasurementOnly one dataset captures the momentary clash between intention and behaviour.
  • PredictionThe gap has only been modelled with random forests: no sequence models and no systematic personalization.
  • InterventionJust-in-time systems decide when to interrupt, not whether the person wants to stop.
  • PopulationThere is no momentary data from Turkey, or from outside the US, Europe and East Asia.
Technology choices

A deliberately modest stack

Part 3 of the report compares sensing APIs, models and deployment options. These are the choices it makes.

Android usage accessUsageStatsManager (Android Developers, n.d.). Events are kept for only a few days, so the app stores its own minimal history.
BaselinesA random forest, as in the paper, plus gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost).
Temporal modelA compact TCN or GRU over recent sessions.
Personal and explainableGlobal, personal and combined variants, with calibration, a daily prompt budget and SHAP explanations (Lundberg & Lee, 2017).
On the phoneLiteRT or ONNX Runtime (Google AI Edge, n.d.; ONNX Runtime, n.d.), inside the existing Kotlin/Compose Eşik app.
Ruled out for now
AccessibilityService: richer data, but it needs Play Store disclosure and approval, and it is weaker on privacy.
iOS Screen Time APIs: built around parental controls, with no raw logs for custom models.
Transformers: they typically need far more data than 5,772 labelled moments.
Cloud inference: easier to update, but it sends behavioural data off the phone.
Data quality and privacy

Known limits, and how we handle them

From Part 2 of the report (Table 4 and Section 2.4).

Target leakage

answer, the remaining_* columns, lock_time and the undocumented temp_scaled (a rescaled copy of the outcome) all reveal the target.

Handling: excluded; features use only what is known at the check-in.

Class imbalance

High-gap moments are 14.9% of check-ins.

Handling: precision–recall AUC and F1, class weights and a tuned threshold; never accuracy alone.

Self-report bias

11 participants answered 10 to more than half of their prompts (up to 89%).

Handling: per-person standardization, personal models, and a sensitivity analysis without near-constant raters.

Missing app information

12.4% of check-ins (717) lack the app category; activity is missing for 2.3%.

Handling: explicit “not recorded” categories with missingness flags; nothing is dropped silently.

Repeated measures

72–294 check-ins per person; 5,549 sessions, 204 of them with more than one check-in.

Handling: leave-one-participant-out splits for new users; chronological splits within each person.

Small, US-only sample

37 adults in five US time zones. Group differences, such as women 19.7% against men 8.1%, rest on 22 and 15 people.

Handling: confidence intervals, per-person results, and explicit limits on transfer to Turkish users.

Privacy by design under KVKK (Law No. 6698, 2016)

  • Explicit opt-in consentBefore any processing, in plain language (Art. 5). An inferred “problematic use” state may count as health data, a special category (Art. 6).
  • Minimal dataApp categories and session timing only: no message or keystroke content and no screen captures (Art. 4).
  • On the deviceProcessing and storage stay on the phone: no account, no advertising use, no sale of data (Art. 12).
  • User controlView, correct, export or delete the data; pause the app or withdraw at any time (Art. 11).
  • Adults onlyMinors are outside the target group; including them would need parental consent and a separate ethics review.
  • Real help, not therapyCrisis-related language leads to Yeşilay YEDAM and the 115 line, never to generated advice.

For AwareMind itself, we report only aggregated results, show no participant codes, keep the raw files local, and use the data for coursework analysis only. OSF lists no licence for the data, so we will obtain written permission from the authors before model training or publication.

What comes next

From review to concept note

Model

Benchmark compact temporal and personalized models of the gap against the published random forest, and report negative results honestly.

Evaluation

Leakage-safe splits: leave-one-participant-out for new users, and chronological splits within each person to test personalization.

Prototype

An opt-in Android prototype built on the Eşik app: quiet by default, a daily prompt budget, and one suggestion from the user’s own list.
